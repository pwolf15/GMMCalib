Analysis of Noise in the Provided Point Clouds
Structural Comparison of Point Clouds
We loaded all three point cloud (PCD) files and compared their structures. The noise-free file (121_frontleftnonoise.pcd) contains 22,705 points, whereas the noisy versions have roughly half as many points (≈12,500 points each). Visually, the overall scene structure is similar across all files – the major shapes and spatial layout are preserved – but the noisy clouds appear more sparse and “fuzzy.” In the noisy point clouds, points are jittered around their original positions and some points are missing, leading to lower density. There are no obvious new geometric structures or patterns introduced by the noise (no extra clusters or rings); the noise does not create a secondary layer of points or systematic offsets, it simply scatters and removes some points. In summary, the noise manifests as a random perturbation and thinning of the cloud, not a coherent distortion of the shape.
Statistical Differences and Noise Characteristics
We computed statistical differences between the noise-free and noisy datasets to characterize the noise. Key observations include:
Point Displacement: Each point in the noisy clouds is displaced only a small amount from the nearest point in the original cloud. For the withnoise001 dataset (which presumably has milder noise), the average displacement is on the order of millimeters (~0.008 m), with a maximum around 0.04 m. For the withnoise dataset (stronger noise), the average displacement is an order of magnitude higher (~0.10 m), with some points shifted up to ~0.76 m. This suggests the noise added is of different magnitudes in the two files – likely corresponding to a small versus a larger Gaussian noise level.
Zero-Mean Jitter: The displacements appear centered around zero. We found no consistent offset in any particular direction – the mean difference in x, y, and z between noisy and original points is essentially zero. This indicates the noise is zero-mean (no systematic bias). In other words, points are equally likely to be perturbed in positive or negative directions, rather than all shifting one way. This is characteristic of random Gaussian noise rather than a calibration offset. Indeed, synthetic LiDAR noise models often assume zero-mean Gaussian errors in range measurements​

Outliers: We looked for any extreme outlier points introduced by noise (points that are completely off any surface). We did not find any obviously spurious outliers. The bounds of the point clouds changed only slightly – for example, the noisy clouds’ minimum and maximum coordinates are within a few centimeters of the originals. The largest vertical deviation was an extra ~9 cm downward in one noisy set, which could indicate one or two ground points fell a bit below the original ground level due to noise, but nothing drastic. No isolated clusters or phantom points were observed. Thus, the noise doesn’t include any non-Gaussian blunders; it’s mostly bounded random jitter.

Effects of Noise on Calibration Performance
The presence of this noise can significantly impact calibration performance. Calibration (for example, extrinsic calibration between sensors or alignment of multiple point clouds) usually relies on geometric correspondences or feature alignment, and noise makes this more challenging in several ways:
Reduced Accuracy: The random jitter (especially in the withnoise case, up to ~10 cm noise) will blur and shift the points on any calibration targets or environmental features. For instance, if you are using planar surfaces or corners for calibration, noise will cause those surfaces to appear rough or the corners to be less sharp. This can introduce errors in estimating calibration parameters. Calibration algorithms often minimize some error metric (like point-to-plane distance in ICP or re-projection error in camera-LiDAR calibration); noise increases those errors and can lead to less precise calibration results. In fact, studies have shown that adding Gaussian noise to point clouds degrades calibration accuracy: one experiment reported that as zero-mean noise increases (up to ~0.14 m std), the translation and rotation errors of calibration solutions also increase (though robust methods can still keep errors bounded)​

. In our case, the noise is zero-mean, so it shouldn’t systematically bias the calibration (no consistent offset in one direction), but it will add variability. You might need more iterations or see a larger residual error after calibration due to this jitter.
Potential for Misalignment: High noise can cause mis-association of features. For example, when aligning two point clouds (say, for LiDAR-LiDAR calibration or odometry), an algorithm like ICP might struggle because noise can make the correspondence between points ambiguous. If noise is very large relative to distinctive features, the optimization might get trapped in a local minimum or fluctuate. The withnoise point cloud, with ~0.1 m noise, could cause an algorithm to slightly mis-align surfaces (particularly if calibration is done on a single frame). The result could be a small rotation or translation error in the final calibration. One paper noted that if a systematic outward shift (bias) is present, it especially harms angular (rotational) calibration – the angular error grew almost linearly with the bias magnitude in their tests​

. In our data we don’t have a systematic bias, which is good; random noise is easier to handle than a biased error. Still, even random noise increases the difficulty of finding the exact alignment.
Lower Robustness: The random removal of nearly half the points means less data to work with for calibration. Fewer points can make calibration solutions less stable, particularly if the missing points remove some reference geometry. If, for example, certain scan lines or patches were removed, the algorithm might give more weight to whatever points remain, potentially skewing the solution if those are not evenly distributed. In our analysis, the removal was uniform, so it shouldn’t bias the calibration to one area – but it does mean any given feature (edge of a wall, etc.) has fewer points describing it. This can make feature detection (like fitting a plane or finding a corner) noisier and less reliable. In essence, the signal-to-noise ratio is reduced both by jitter and by sparse sampling.
Calibration Error and Repeatability: In practice, calibration performed on noisy data will exhibit higher residual errors and possibly less repeatability. If one were to calibrate using the noise-free cloud vs the noisy cloud, the noisy one might yield calibration parameters that deviate slightly from ground truth and have a larger uncertainty. . This means that without countermeasures, noise can cause calibration to deteriorate. Additionally, if one uses an optimization-based calibration, noise can introduce more local minima or jitter in the cost function, requiring more robust strategies to converge correctly.

The noise will not systematically throw off the calibration (since it’s zero-mean, it won’t consistently shift the sensor pose estimate in one direction), but it will inject uncertainty. Calibration algorithms need to be robust to outliers and noise to still perform well.
Suggested Preprocessing Steps to Mitigate Noise
To improve calibration results, it’s wise to preprocess the point clouds to reduce noise impact. Here are some recommended steps:
Statistical Outlier Removal (SOR): Apply a statistical outlier filter to remove any spurious points that are far from their neighbors. This technique computes the average distance from each point to its k nearest neighbors and removes points that deviate beyond a chosen threshold (e.g., 2–3 standard deviations from the mean distance)​

. In our case, we didn’t see dramatic outliers, but SOR can still trim the extreme tails of the noise distribution. This will eliminate any isolated noise points and slightly denoise edges of surfaces. It’s a conservative way to clean the cloud without knowing the exact noise model.
Radius Outlier Removal: Similarly, a radius filter can be used – any point that has fewer than N neighbors within a certain radius is removed as an outlier​
OPEN3D.ORG
. This can help in uniformly sparse noise situations. For instance, if random dropout left some single, isolated points, this filter would catch those. Tuning the radius to a value slightly larger than the expected point spacing on real surfaces can weed out points that are unnaturally isolated (likely noise).
Downsampling and Smoothing: You might downsample the point cloud using a voxel grid or other methods, not necessarily to reduce data (since it’s already sparse) but to aggregate points and average out noise. A voxel grid filter can merge points within each cell, effectively averaging their coordinates. This can act as a simple smoothing: jittered points on a surface may be replaced by a single point at the average location, which is closer to the true surface. Care is needed to choose a voxel size small enough to preserve calibration-relevant details (maybe on the order of the noise magnitude or slightly above). Another smoothing approach is to fit local planes or use moving least squares on the point cloud to slide points onto a smoother surface. These methods reduce random scatter.
Segmentation of Calibration Targets: If your calibration uses specific target structures (like checkerboards, planes, or corners), you can fit models to those regions in the noisy cloud. For example, if a planar calibration board is present in the cloud, rather than taking the raw points (which are noisy) you could do a plane fit to those points. This yields a much more accurate estimate of that plane’s orientation/position than any single noisy point. Essentially, use a model-fitting approach to get robust features from noisy data. This mitigates point jitter by leveraging the fact that the underlying geometry (a flat board, etc.) is known. Even without dedicated targets, you can detect large planar patches (walls, ground) in the environment and fit planes – these fitted entities will be less affected by noise and can be used for calibration.
Multiple Frame Averaging: If possible, collect multiple frames and average or combine them. Noise is random, so by averaging many independent noisy observations, you approach the true shape. For instance, if you can accumulate point clouds (in a common frame) from multiple time steps, the random noise will average out (the mean of noise tends to zero). The densification from multiple frames also counteracts the random dropout (points missing in one frame might appear in another). A well-calibrated system could use such aggregated point clouds to refine calibration.
Robust Calibration Algorithms: Use calibration methods that are designed to handle noise and outliers. Many point cloud registration algorithms offer robust loss functions (kernels) that down-weight the influence of outlier correspondences​

. For example, when performing ICP (Iterative Closest Point) alignment, you can use a robust cost (like Cauchy or Tukey loss) so that if a point’s correspondence error is large (possibly due to noise or a wrong match), it doesn’t wreck the solution. This makes the calibration solution less sensitive to noise spikes. Additionally, increase the neighbor search radius or correspondence threshold slightly to account for noise jitter​

 – since points moved a bit, the algorithm should still consider a match if it’s within, say, 20 cm instead of 5 cm of the model, given we expect up to ~10 cm noise. By combining a tolerant matching criteria with robust outlier rejection, the calibration can succeed despite noise.
Validate and Refine: After an initial calibration on the noisy data, validate the result and consider refining with a smaller step size. Sometimes running a second pass of calibration (or a refinement step that only fine-tunes the solution) can help, as the largest misalignments may be corrected in the first pass, leaving only small residual errors that can be further optimized. This is essentially an iterative refinement, which some calibration methods include by default (e.g., bundle adjustment style refinement)​

. With noisy data, a single shot solution might not be optimal, but iterative refinement can converge toward the true parameters if outliers are handled.
In conclusion, to handle the noise before or during calibration, one should filter out outliers, smooth random jitter, and use algorithms robust to noise. The goal is to ensure the calibration algorithm sees as clean and truthful a geometric structure as possible. By removing the most egregious noise effects (isolated points, heavy scatter) and accounting for the remaining uncertainty in the algorithm, you can achieve calibration results nearly as good as with the noise-free data.
