Vertical structures or any object protruding from the ground create edges or corners with the ground, which help constrain yaw and horizontal translations. Influence of Dataset Structure on GMMCalib Performance
The structure of the dataset – meaning the arrangement and shape of the overlapping points – strongly influences how well GMMCalib can optimize the alignment. GMMCalib works by modeling the point clouds with Gaussian mixtures and finding the transform that best aligns these distributions. If the point distribution covers the environment broadly (as in the default dataset), the resulting Gaussian components represent various distinct parts of the scene. This helps the algorithm avoid ambiguous solutions. In a simple structured scene (like the cubes), the Gaussian components might end up representing just a few clusters (each cube and the ground). Fewer components can lead to ambiguities where different transforms yield similar overlap. For example, a known issue in registration is that if features are symmetrically placed, a rotation error can be “compensated” by a translation error, resulting in a nearly overlapping alignment​

Why the Default Dataset Performs Better
Combining all the above factors, the default dataset yields better calibration accuracy because it offers more overlap information and redundant constraints for the algorithm. Its high point density over large, varied surfaces means the calibration is solving a heavily over-constrained problem – there is essentially only one way to align all those points correctly. Any error in orientation or position would show up as misalignment of big, obvious features (e.g. the horizon of the ground would split or a building edge would double). The cube dataset, while providing the minimum needed features, makes the calibration a more weakly constrained problem. Fewer points and features mean the solution isn’t uniquely “forced” by the data – small errors can hide in the blind spots of the dataset. The presence of more noise relative to signal and potential partial overlaps further worsens the precision for the cube data. In summary, the default dataset’s broad coverage, variety of structures, and dense data give GMMCalib a strong, unambiguous signal to lock onto, resulting in higher accuracy. The cube dataset’s sparse, localized points and simple geometry give a weaker signal with more room for uncertainty, leading to lower calibration accuracy.

Alternatively, using a higher resolution LiDAR or placing the cubes closer to the sensors can increase point density per surface.
